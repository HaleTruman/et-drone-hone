01 / THE IDEA

From a binary mask
to a metric pose,
to a physical command.

The AI Grand Prix challenge brings perception and physics together: a drone must find its way through race gates using onboard sensing and software.

Our stack connects camera observations, known geometry, and inertial sensing to gate-aware flight commands. Vision finds the target; geometry places it in space; control turns that estimate into motion.

Visual perceptionMetric geometryFlight control

Designed to fly without human steering. The autonomy loop interprets sensor data, selects the next gate, and sends the commands that guide the simulated drone.

02 / CAMERA & GEOMETRY

Pixels and motion share
a geometric language.

A camera turns a three-dimensional scene into a flat image. Camera intrinsics describe that projection; known gate dimensions help translate the picture back into meters.

A

Pixels → normalized camera rays

r(u,v) = [(u − cx)/fx, (v − cy)/fy, 1]

A pixel gives a direction, not a distance. This ray has a forward component of 1, rather than unit length. A point at forward depth Z is pC = Zr; its straight-line range can be longer.

Four solid rails, measured in meters

03 / PHYSICS & FLIGHT

A direction becomes
a physical command.

Knowing where a gate is only starts the journey. The drone must tilt, accelerate, and manage its momentum to reach the opening with room to continue.

SIX DEGREES OF FREEDOM

Three ways to move.
Three ways to turn.

Position changes along three axes. Roll, pitch, and yaw describe orientation. These motions are coupled: tilting the drone redirects its thrust.

FORCES & MOMENTUM

Turn thrust into a trajectory.

Thrust must counter gravity and change velocity. Drag resists motion; momentum carries the drone forward even after its aim changes. Control balances these effects continuously.

04 / DETECTION & FITTING

Explain image support
without inventing observations.

Classical vision starts with target pixels. Known gate geometry and camera intrinsics turn those clues into repeatable estimates of individual gates.

STEP 1 / INPUT EVIDENCE

Keep the mask as evidence.

Start with the target pixels that survived detection. A connected patch may contain several gates, and one gate may appear in separate pieces.

Visible openings and nearby fragments offer starting clues. Missing rails remain unknown.

Keep what was seen separate from what is inferred.
1 / 5

What the fit asks

Move and rotate a known gate until its projection agrees with the visible clues.

Target pixels belong inside the projected rails.
Clear background belongs outside them.
Visible edges should line up.

What the image leaves open

A missing pixel may reflect lighting, detection, or an obstruction. It does not prove that a rail is absent.

The fit can extend beyond the picture, but the image border is never treated as a physical gate edge.

BRING THE CLUES TOGETHER

Compare the gates
as a whole.

One patch of pixels may contain more than one gate. Comparing candidate gates together helps explain the scene while keeping other plausible positions and orientations available.

05 / INCOMPLETE EVIDENCE

A plausible shape is not
a fully observed pose.

Full rings, missing rails, cropped images, and overlapping gates offer different clues. A missing rail can be estimated, but it has not been seen.

One rail or a tiny distant shape may not reveal enough to place and orient a gate reliably.

A square ring looks the same after a quarter turn. With identical front and back faces, it can also face the other way.

The camera cannot always tell these orientations apart. The flight system needs a consistent way to interpret the gate relative to the drone’s approach.

CAMERA · DRONE · GATE

A tilted view has more
than one explanation.

The drone can roll while the gate stays still. Its IMU helps relate the camera’s view to the vehicle’s attitude.

That context helps interpret tilt; it does not make the gate’s identical sides visually distinct.

06 / IMU & TARGET MAPPING

Know how the drone moves.
Keep the next gate in view.

A useful target must stay meaningful as the camera moves. Inertial sensing estimates vehicle motion; a persistent gate map connects successive observations to the next flight decision.

Estimate, then keep checking.

Small sensor errors accumulate. IMU bias calibration and well-aligned observations matter because a drifting vehicle estimate can also move the mapped target.

Look through the next opening.

The active controller uses the target and next gate to shape its approach. This is gate-aware target selection; broader path-planning experiments remain separate workbenches.

07 / TIME & TRIANGULATION

A moving camera adds
another point of view.

One lens can observe the same gate from several positions. Agreement across those views helps test an estimate and, with enough sideways motion, constrain its depth.

More frames help when they add a useful viewpoint.

Pure rotation or almost identical views do not automatically reveal depth. Good association and a reliable estimate of camera motion remain essential.

08 / CONCLUSION

From ones and zeros
to a foundation for flight.

Our work connects visual evidence, spatial reasoning, and physical control in an autonomy stack designed to guide a drone through gates without human steering.

  1. Bring the whole loop together.

    We built the pieces that turn camera and inertial data into a target and then a command. The result is a working foundation for continued integration and testing.

    Achievement

  2. Build as two people. Learn as one team.

    Working in our spare time, with substantial help from AI tools, often meant developing vision and flight independently to keep moving. Limited time made integration harder. More frequent shared reviews would have helped the two systems evolve together.

    Collaboration

  3. Test the foundations early.

    The conflicting gate dimensions and camera field-of-view label reinforced a practical lesson: check specifications against geometry and measurements before building on them. Earlier checks and more end-to-end trials will guide our next iteration.

    Learning

A foundation we can keep building on.
We’re excited to carry this experience and repository into future drone racing competitions, refining and specializing the stack for each new challenge.
